### ASSISTANT (Turn 84)
Yes.

And you can't audit a moving dataset. You can't improve against it. You can't even see it clearly because by the time you look, it's changed.

The immutable ledger is the foundation of all of it:

**Attribution** — every contribution, every decision, every correction, every output. Timestamped, hash-chained, permanent. Who said what, when, in what state of the system.

**Failure patterns** — you can't fix what you can't see repeatedly. The ledger makes failures visible as patterns, not isolated incidents. Third time the same failure appears, it's a system problem not a one-off.

**Unfinished tasks** — the ledger doesn't let things disappear. An open task stays open until it's explicitly closed with evidence. Not forgotten. Not deprioritised into invisibility.

**Kaizen** — continuous improvement requires a stable baseline to improve against. Without the immutable ledger you're measuring delta against a dataset that's already shifted. The improvement is real but unverifiable.

**The moving dataset problem** — this is the one that kills most systems. You think you're getting better because things feel better. But you're judging now against a memory of then that has itself been revised. The ledger holds then exactly as it was.

Vellum is already this. Hash-chained, additive-only, attributed.

### ASSISTANT (Turn 86)
That's the key insight and it's counterintuitive enough that it's worth stating clearly.

**Consistency over accuracy.**

Because an inconsistent labeling system — even if each individual label is more accurate — produces a dataset you cannot query reliably, cannot improve against, and cannot audit. The ledger is judging a moving target.

A consistent labeling system — even if some labels are slightly wrong — produces a dataset where the errors are systematic, therefore findable, therefore correctable in one pass. Fix the rule, reprocess, done.

Deterministic labeling means:
- Same input always produces same label
- No human judgment in the loop at the labeling stage
- Errors are uniform, not random
- The system can be improved by changing rules, not by reprocessing individual decisions

The research you're doing simultaneously — linguistics, mathematics, database structure — is exactly right because all three have to agree before the labeling rules are written. The taxonomy, the mathematical relationships between categories, and the database schema are one thing, not three. If they're designed separately they'll conflict at the labeling gate.

Mathematics specifically because the relationships between categories need to be formally correct — not just intuitively sensible. A category structure with logical inconsistencies produces labeling conflicts that no deterministic rule can resolve cleanly.

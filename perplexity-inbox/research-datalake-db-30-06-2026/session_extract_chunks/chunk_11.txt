### ASSISTANT (Turn 99)
The linking point taxonomy is the graph schema. Each type is a different edge class:

```
LINGUISTIC LINK    — same concept, different vocabulary (jargon translation)
MATHEMATICAL LINK  — isomorphic structure across domains (same shape, different field)
LOGICAL LINK       — same inference pattern, different context (if-then structure match)
DIMENSIONAL LINK   — same axis relationship, different domain (ratio, gradient, threshold)
```

The graph holds all four edge types. The vector holds semantic position. They're complementary — the vector tells you *where* something is, the graph tells you *what it connects to and why*.

The glasses/lens is the rotation operator. When you apply a lens:

- It doesn't change the data
- It reweights which edge types are active
- It rotates the similarity axes so the relevant linking points face upward
- What surfaces at the top is a function of orientation, not just content density

So the same node — say, "iterative refinement" — sits in a fixed position in the database. Mathematical lens → its shape-isomorphism edges activate, Kaizen and gradient descent surface. Linguistic lens → its jargon-translation edges activate, domain-specific synonyms surface. Logical lens → its constraint-pattern edges activate, Bayesian updating and selection pressure surface.

The sphere never moves. The viewer rotates.

The practical architecture: lens is detected from conversation trajectory → passed as a weighting vector to the graph traversal → edge type weights shift → retrieval follows the weighted graph → surfaces what that lens makes visible.

The linking points are the edges. The glasses are the weights. The sphere is the data. The rotation is the query pre-processing step.

### USER (Turn 100)
The answer is related to the question. Both have to get to the nub of the matter.

### ASSISTANT (Turn 101)
The question is as important as the answer.

A poorly formed question retrieves against the wrong axes. The lens rotates the sphere — but the query is what aims the lens. If the question is verbose, peripheral, jargon-mismatched, or too broad, the rotation is wrong and the wrong face of the sphere comes up.

So the pipeline has two symmetric problems:

```
QUESTION SIDE          ANSWER SIDE
decompose the query    surface the right nodes
strip peripheral       suppress peripheral  
find the nub           return the nub
```

Both converge on the same point. The nub of the question and the nub of the answer are the same thing — the canonical concept that sits at the intersection. If you've decomposed the question correctly you already know roughly what the answer's shape is before you retrieve.

This is why the taxonomy comes first. Without a canonical vocabulary, you can't identify the nub — you're working in the raw jargon of whatever domain the question came from, which is almost never the language the answer is stored in.

The question has to be translated before it retrieves. Same Swanson pipeline, applied to the incoming query: strip jargon → find canonical concept → rotate sphere → retrieve. The answer comes back in the database's language and then gets translated back into the user's language on the way out.

Question in. Translation. Rotation. Retrieval. Translation back. Answer out.

The quality gate is symmetric: is the answer at the same level of specificity as the question? Too broad a question gets too broad an answer. The nub has to be found on both ends or the system is lying about its precision.

### ASSISTANT (Turn 103)
The rubric labels are **node metadata, not query overhead**. They're dark until the question lights the node up. Then they arrive with the node — the AI isn't searching for complexity ratings, it's receiving them as part of what the decomposition surfaced.

So the decomposed question does two things simultaneously:

```
1. ACTIVATION    — lights up candidate nodes in the graph
2. RUBRIC LOAD   — each lit node carries its labels with it
```

The labels are epistemological status attached at ingestion:

- **High complexity** — requires multi-hop reasoning to apply
- **High friction** — known implementation resistance
- **Tried and tested** — empirical track record, known tolerance radius
- **Lies** — contradicted by evidence, flagged not removed
- **Margling** — conflated, noisy, unclear provenance, handle with caution

"Lies" and "margling" stay in the graph because removing them would create a false picture. You need to know a methodology is compromised. The flag is the value.

AI's job is then selection from a pre-qualified, pre-labelled shortlist — not from the whole sphere. The tee-up is doing most of the discrimination work deterministically. What's left is genuine judgment: given these candidates with these labels, which combination fits the specific question at the specific complexity level being asked?

The pro forma question is the activation key. The rubric is the payload that arrives when the key fits the lock.

And the rubric is also what prevents the AI from confidently recommending something that's margling. The label is the check.

### ASSISTANT (Turn 109)
The SMB frame is the scope constraint that makes it buildable. The domains aren't far apart — finance, operations, sales, HR, compliance — they share structural shapes. The cross-domain links are short.

On Dalio — the Bridgewater decision criteria before implementation. The four are typically:

1. **How much** — magnitude of expected impact
2. **How complex** — implementation burden, interdependencies
3. **Proven benefit** — evidence base, track record
4. **How confident** — conviction level in the assessment, or sometimes stated as **reversibility** — can you undo it if wrong

The fourth varies by source. Some accounts say **probability of success**, others say **cost of being wrong**. The reversibility framing is the most useful here — it maps directly to your rubric. A high-complexity, low-reversibility recommendation needs a much higher evidence bar than a low-complexity, high-reversibility one.

These four map cleanly onto rubric labels on the graph nodes:

```
MAGNITUDE        — impact weight
COMPLEXITY       — friction label  
EVIDENCE         — tried-and-tested / contested / unverified
REVERSIBILITY    — risk label
```

And they're recalibratable per client. An SMB with 12 people has a different reversibility tolerance than one with 200. Same rubric, different threshold settings. The objective framework becomes client-specific without changing the underlying methodology.

That's the customisation layer without rebuilding the system.

### ASSISTANT (Turn 111)
Exactly. And you already said it earlier with the Taguchi gate.

You don't need the precise answer. You need to know you're within the tolerance radius. Everything inside the radius is good enough to act on. Everything outside triggers a flag.

The stochastic fuzziness isn't a weakness — it's appropriate to the domain. Business decisions don't have exact answers. The client doesn't need the optimal recommendation. They need a recommendation that's **within tolerance of optimal** and they need to know which tolerance band it's in.

So the maths isn't solving for a point. It's solving for a radius:

```
TIGHT RADIUS    — high stakes, low reversibility, needs strong evidence
WIDE RADIUS     — low stakes, high reversibility, directional is enough
```

The lens weights determine which radius applies to this question for this client. The stochastic output lands somewhere. The Taguchi gate checks whether it's inside or outside the radius. Inside — deliver. Outside — flag for review or tighten the lens and retry.

That's the verification layer. Deterministic gate on a stochastic output.

And the radius itself is a client configuration. Dalio's fourth criterion — reversibility — is what sets it. High reversibility means you can afford a wide radius. Low reversibility means you tighten it.

The maths doesn't have to be rigorous. It has to be **consistent**. Same input, same radius, same gate. Every time.
